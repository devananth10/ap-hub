| Suffix | Particle and alternative checks |
| --- | --- |
| 001 | Fixed repeating sites/vibration → crystalline solid; close/change neighbors → liquid; sparse/container-wide travel → gas. Other label permutations fail the observations. |
| 002 | A nonperiodic shape-retaining material with restricted translation on the given timescale fits amorphous solid; lack of order alone does not prove liquid/gas. |
| 003 | Similar close contacts explain similar solid/liquid molar volumes, versus much larger gas separations. Molecular mass/identity/size do not change merely with phase. No universal solid-denser-than-liquid claim. |
| 004 | Neighbor changes permit liquid flow; close contacts preserve approximate volume. Not a fixed lattice, container-shaped individual molecules, or room-filling gas. |
| 005 | Expansion conserves intact molecular identities/count while changing accessible volume and spacing. Does not enlarge molecules, create matter, or destroy diluted particles. |
| 006 | A solid above zero temperature is not motionless; show vibration about sites rather than free streaming, force removal, or continuous molecular growth. |
| 007 | Gas compression mostly reduces large interparticle spaces; liquids have close contacts and are much less compressible, not exactly incompressible. No universal liquid covalent network or chemical destruction. |
| 008 | Ten N2 molecules become ten separated N2 molecules. Twenty atoms conserve N count but dissociate; five pairs lose matter; ten triples add matter. |
| 009 | Static disorder cannot alone distinguish an amorphous solid from a liquid. Time-resolved rearrangement/translation provides relevant evidence; a particle count or atomic identity alone does not. Observation timescale explicitly matters. |
| 010 | Dense liquid and dilute vapor contain the same molecular species, not unbonded vapor atoms, equal close-packing, or two fixed lattices. Phase coexistence is represented without a phase diagram. |
| 011 | Open crystal increases volume per unchanged molecular mass, lowering density. Solid need not always be denser; no loss of particles or decrease in individual molecular mass is required. |
